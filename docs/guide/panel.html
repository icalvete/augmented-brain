The panel

The panel is a page in your browser to see and change your spaces without going through Claude. Open it with /brain:panel in Claude, or from a terminal:

augmented-brain panel

It opens your browser on Today, with the space of the folder you ran it in. From a terminal it also prints an address, in case the browser did not open (on a computer with no screen, say). That address opens the panel once, within 2 minutes; run the command again for another. When Claude runs it for you, it only opens the browser: Claude never sees an address that opens the panel. If the browser cannot open (Claude working in the background, a computer with no screen), it prints no address either and asks you to run the command in a terminal of your own.

The panel changes your notes the same way Claude does, with the same program, and they wait for each other: you can have both open at once.

Every page starts with a few words on what it is and what it is for, and a link to its part of this guide. Once you know your way around, Hide this help hides it on that page, and Hide it on every page on all of them. The panel remembers it on this computer; What is this page? brings it back.

Spaces and pages

At the side, under Profile, the Space list picks the space you are looking at. Some pages show only that space; others show all of them, or this computer as a whole. The menu has three blocks: Day to day, Notes and Upkeep. When the window is too short for it, the side scrolls on its own, so Stop the panel is always within reach. On a phone, the menu folds under the name of the page you are on; open, it shows each block in two columns, and a shadow at its foot says there is more below.

Today

The first page: every space at once. For each one:

  • what is due in the next 7 days;
  • the tasks waiting on you (the parked ones in one line);
  • how many captures wait in the inbox;
  • whether the weekly review is due;
  • the sessions that were cut off, each with Keep it in the notes and Mark as reviewed (see Sessions);
  • notes with problems.

Below, Repositories: those your open tasks name in repos: that hold changes not committed or commits not pushed. That is work that is only on this computer. One whose tasks are all parked (or left for some day) is marked “forgotten?”, here and on the whole map; a clean one never is. A branch never pushed counts only if the repository has a remote (a copy on a server, such as GitHub) to push it to. See all repositories leads to the whole map.

Tasks

Grouped by area, those waiting on you first, with their status and next step. Filters show only those waiting on you, under way, parked, untouched, or waiting on one person. A task nobody has changed for 14 days is marked “untouched N days”. At the end, folded, the tasks left for Someday and the Closed ones.

Open one. Its essentials come first, then its steps and what you can do with it: write in its journal, change its status or next step, tick its steps, park it or close it. Below, the other sections folded, and the journal newest first. You can open new tasks too. If a status skips phases, the journal names them. When a task waits on a person you have a note of, “waits on” leads to them.

Closing a task here does what closing it in a session does: if the task has a session of its own, that session ends once it finishes its answer, and its worktree goes when it holds nothing of its own.

To open a closed task again, find it under Closed and choose a new status for it; its journal says it was reopened from the panel.

When you save a task, only what you changed on the page is saved. If Claude changed something else in the meantime, that stays. A step is ticked only if it still says what the page showed.

Sessions

Your Claude sessions on this computer, from the last 30 days. It is in the menu, under Day to day; Health and a cut-off session on Today also lead to it.

  • Those that did not end (closed by force, or cut off when the computer went down) show what was said after the last journal entry. Keep it in the notes (it goes to archive/sessions/ with a line in the task's journal) or mark it as reviewed.
  • Those running now, and those that ended, with how much disk their transcripts take (the files where Claude Code keeps each conversation). You can delete the transcript of an ended session; that session can then no longer be resumed.

Reminders

The watch dates of your tasks (closed ones too) and the inbox reminders: overdue first, then the next 30 days. Mark one done, or add a watch date to a task. A reminder that belongs to no task is a capture with a date: make it in the Inbox.

Inbox

Capture an idea, with a date to be reminded if you want one. Mark one as dealt with, or make it a task.

Weekly review

The weekly review, on a page. Each capture becomes a task, a reference or a lesson, or is dropped. Each task untouched for two weeks goes on as it is, or goes to some day (or you open it to close it). What you decide is noted as you go, and kept in the review's record when you finish.

Areas, knowledge, rules and people

Knowledge and rules show the space's own and the shared ones, marked “shared”. Here you read them; you do not write them. A reference or a lesson comes from Claude, or from a capture in the weekly review; a rule, from Claude. You can create areas and change an area's title and summary.

People are listed once, with a badge for each space they are in. A new person starts in the space you are in. A person's page is one form: the name stays (a new name would be a new person), then a role and other names for each space, then a list of details. Each detail has a box per space; tick where it should show. Empty a detail's text to remove it. A detail with no box ticked is not saved, and the page says so next to it. There is always an empty row at the end to add one, and “Another detail” reloads the page with one more (save first: it does not keep what you typed). The page also shows the tasks that wait on them and those that link to them.

Repositories

Your map of repositories: every git repository in the work folders of each space, up to three folders down. It skips folders inside another repository, hidden folders, node_modules and vendor. They are grouped by space, those with work pending first.

For each one: its branch, where it pushes (as host/owner/name, never with a password), files changed, commits not pushed, the open tasks that name it, and one line of yours on what it is. Write that line right there. It is kept in the space's notes, in the reference “Repositories” (knowledge/ref-repositories.md), so the backup keeps it. Claude can write it too.

The panel only reads your repositories. Git runs without taking locks, so it never gets in your way, and it never asks for a password. It only runs inside the work folders, and links to folders outside them are not followed.

Secrets

The names of the secrets you keep in your system keychain (where your computer keeps passwords), never their values. Each one as service/name, what it is for, and which tasks name it. So you know which there are, and a new machine knows which to ask you for.

Check the keychain says whether each one is on this computer. It asks secret-tool on Linux and security on macOS; elsewhere it cannot tell. It asks only when you click, because a locked keychain asks you for its password. When the keychain lacks one, the page shows the command that stores it (that command asks you for the value). When the keychain cannot be asked (on Linux with no desktop session, or a locked keychain on macOS), the secret shows “cannot tell here”, not missing: check it there before storing it again.

You can add a name, or take one off the list (the secret stays in the keychain). Anything that looks like a value is refused. The list is the same in every space, and lives in secrets.toml (see Configuration).

Backup

Whether the notes of each space have a copy elsewhere, in a private repository, so they survive if this computer breaks. A space with no backup says so in red.

  • For a space with a backup: how the last copy went, where it goes, the last copies with the date and the computer that made them, and Back up now. The copy runs in the background and takes a few seconds; reload the page to see how it went. It also runs by itself every time a session ends.
  • Each copy shows What it changed, note by note, with the lines. Go back to this copy shows first what would change in your notes now, and asks for a box and the space's name. What you had is kept as a copy first, so it can be undone. See Go back to a copy.
  • For a space with none: Set it up on my GitHub makes a private repository called augmented-brain-<space> in your GitHub account and the first copy. It needs the GitHub command line, gh, installed and logged in; the page says how if it is not. I already have a private repository takes its address instead.
  • Bring the notes back on another computer shows the command to run there. Restoring is never done from the panel: it is for a computer that does not have the notes yet.

Moving a backup that exists somewhere else is done in a terminal, with augmented-brain backup setup. From another device the page only shows. See Backup.

Health

The space's notes folder and work folders, whether it is the default space, notes that are not valid, repositories that notes point at and that are no longer on this computer, the index, the last backup, the last weekly review, and a link to the space's Sessions.

Guard

Every list the guard answers to, in one page, to read and change:

  • Untouchable repositories, where Claude never commits, pushes or merges. Removing one warns that the other lists decide again for it (if one lets Claude commit and push there, it will), and asks you to type the folder's name.
  • Repositories each space lets Claude commit in, or ask you about, with a form to add or remove. The list where Claude commits and pushes without asking carries a warning, and adding to it asks you to tick that you understand.
  • Root exceptions, with the date each ends (the ones that ended are marked). Adding one warns what it allows and asks you to type the machine's name again.
  • Production profiles and untouchable machines.
  • The model of your sessions (preferred_model in session.toml): when a session answers with another one, Claude tells you. Below it, what Claude Code said about its model at the last session start.
  • What the assistant saves on its own (memory_trust in session.toml): whether Claude saves a rule, your profile and additions to the guard's lists and you are told, or asks you first. The guard tells it.
  • The files Claude cannot change on its own.

How it behaves:

  • Your comments in config.toml stay where they are.
  • If the lists are not the ones you last saw (something changed them outside this page), a notice says what was added and what was removed, list by list, compared with the lists you last accepted. When those are no longer among the kept versions, it says so.
  • Then a button, I have seen them, accept. Until you press it, or start a new session, a commit, push or merge that the lists allow asks you first.
  • Whatever you change or accept here, Claude Code tells you in your open sessions, after the next answer or message, in a line that starts with “augmented brain · the guard's lists”. If you did not do it, look here.
  • Claude cannot open this page: it never sees the panel's address, and the guard denies it the panel's key.
  • From another device the page only shows. The lists decide what Claude may do on this computer, so they change only from this computer's panel.

How each list works is in The guard.

Versions

The last 10 versions of your configuration (spaces, guard, remote access, session settings, profile and your secrets' names), kept only on this computer, newest first, with when and who changed it. What it changed shows the lines of each one; Go back to this one makes it the configuration again, keeping the one you had as a version first. It asks you to tick a box, and warns first when the guard's lists would change, with what. They are never in the backup: your notes are, with their own history. From another device the page only shows. See Configuration versions.

Help

Links to the answers on this website, in your language. And a way to tell us about a problem or propose an idea on GitHub (the website where the project lives; you need a free account there).

Click Report a problem on GitHub. The panel runs the health check (what augmented-brain doctor says) at that moment. Then it opens GitHub's form in a new tab, with the version, your system and that check filled in.

Before it opens, the panel takes out what is personal: your home folder (shown as ~), your notes and work folders, e-mail addresses, the names of your spaces, untouchable machines, production AWS profiles, and any line that looks like a password or a key. You write what happened, and read the rest: change or delete what you prefer not to share. Nothing is sent until you press GitHub's submit button. To be honest about it: the text travels to GitHub inside the page's address as soon as it opens, so GitHub can see it then, even if you never submit.

Profile

At the top of the side, above the space list (on a phone, last in the menu). Who you are and how you like to work: the same profile every space uses.

Under Your assistant you can give Claude a name and a personality. Every session starts with them. They change how it talks, never the rules, the guard or its honesty. With a name, every session asks Claude to sign the emails it writes in your name with that name and, under it, a line such as “Ana's assistant (AI, Claude)”, so whoever reads it knows no person wrote it. The program writes that line, not you, so you cannot leave it out; Claude is the one who adds it to the email. The page shows the signature. Leave both fields empty for no name and no personality.

Search and reading notes

The search box at the side looks through the notes of the space and the shared ones: titles, summaries and text. A note shows up when it holds every word, in any case, with accents or without.

Notes read as text, not as raw Markdown (the plain-text format notes are written in): headings, lists, tables, code and links. A [[link]] to another note takes you to it. If a note changes on disk while its page is open (Claude wrote in it, say), the page tells you, with a link to see it as it is now.

Made to stay yours

  • It runs only while you use it. After 30 minutes without use it stops by itself. To stop it now: augmented-brain panel stop, or the link at the bottom of the page.
  • It works only on this computer. Other machines on your network cannot reach it. To open it from your phone or another computer of yours, use augmented-brain panel remote: see The panel from another device.
  • The address you get opens it once. The panel then takes your browser to its pages, whose address carries a key, made new each time the panel starts. Without it the panel answers nothing, so another program or web page on your computer cannot use it. Treat the address of the pages like a password: do not share it. It stops working when the panel stops. If something else used your link first, your browser says “This link was already used” instead of opening: you know.
  • On Linux the browser gets a small file, readable only by you, that takes it to the address, instead of the address itself: what a program is started with can be read by every user of the computer. When your default browser is a snap or a flatpak (the Firefox that comes with Ubuntu is a snap), it cannot read that file, and gets the address as before; so do the browsers of macOS.
  • It loads nothing from the internet: no fonts, no statistics. It works offline. A note cannot put HTML or scripts of its own in the page. The links of the Help page go to the website only when you click them.
  • Checking whether a note changed does not count as using the panel: a tab left open does not keep it running.
